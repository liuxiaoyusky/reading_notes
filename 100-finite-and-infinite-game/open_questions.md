# Open Questions

This file records unsettled doubts and objections while reading *Finite and Infinite Games*. These are not final notes. Move or summarize them into `notes.md` only after the question is clarified and accepted as settled understanding.

## 2026-06-07 - Does single-player play challenge "we cannot play alone"?

**Status**

Unsettled.

**Question**

Carse says:

> It is the case that we cannot play if we must play, but it is also the case *that we cannot play alone.*

But single-player games seem to be a direct counterexample. If someone plays a single-player video game, solitaire, a puzzle, or a self-imposed challenge, they are plainly "playing" in ordinary language. So what exactly does Carse mean by `game` and `play` here?

**Current best reading**

The strongest current interpretation is:

Carse may not mean that a person can never play without another human physically present. He may mean that a finite game cannot be created and validated by one person's private wish alone. A finite game still depends on some recognized structure: rules, boundaries, goals, winning/losing conditions, a designed system, a tradition, or some form of external validation.

Under that reading, a single-player video game can still be a finite game because the game system supplies rules, boundaries, obstacles, and success/failure conditions. The player is alone at the controls, but not alone in defining what counts as the game.

**Why this remains doubtful**

This explanation may be too charitable. The sentence says "we cannot play alone," and the following examples emphasize opponents, teammates, and recognized professional membership. That sounds more social than merely rule-based. Single-player games may therefore expose a weakness or ambiguity in Carse's wording.

**What to watch for later**

- Does Carse later define `play` in a way that requires social recognition?
- Does he distinguish ordinary private amusement from finite/infinite play?
- Does he treat puzzles, art, solitaire, private practice, or solitary activity as play?
- Does "alone" mean no other person, or no shared rule/world/recognition?

**Provisional takeaway**

Do not treat "we cannot play alone" as fully resolved yet. For now, read it as a claim about the social or rule-governed validation of finite games, while keeping single-player games as a live objection.
